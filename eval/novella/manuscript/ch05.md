# Chapter Five — Keeping It Dark

Nore told it plainly, the way you tell a thing you have carried too long to dress up.

For a year they had made her father keep the light dark on the new-moon nights — the men who owned the boats that came through the Teeth, and the villagers who took a share and asked no questions, which was to say the island. Aldous had gone along. A keeper who wouldn't wouldn't keep, and a keeper who wouldn't keep had a way of going gone.

Then a passenger ship strayed toward the coast on a moonless night, off her course in fog, trusting the great guarded rocks of Halvis to be lit and warning. And the wreckers hung out their lure on the Teeth to bring her in.

Aldous lit the lighthouse.

He climbed his cold dark tower with the arrangement a year deep on his hands and he lit the lamp full and white and threw it across the water, and the ship saw the true light and the false one and chose the true, and lived. That was the light the whole coast swore they'd seen. His last light. He wrote the false line in the log first — *keeping it dark, as agreed* — so that when they came for him it would read, to them, like obedience, and they would not think to go looking for a daughter who might have been told. Then he lit it anyway. They took him off the north horn before the ship was even out of the bay.

"Where," said Lin. It was not quite a question.

"The Teeth," Nore said. "Where else. They gave him to the rocks he wouldn't give the ship to." She stood. "You wanted the truth. Now you're the keeper who knows it. The light's yours, keeper. So is the choice he made. New moon's in twenty-nine days."

Lin thought of the warm brass that had only ever held the memory of a flame, and the tin strip nailed to her door in a bright new hand — *Aldous kept watch* — and understood at last that it was not an epitaph. It was an instruction.
